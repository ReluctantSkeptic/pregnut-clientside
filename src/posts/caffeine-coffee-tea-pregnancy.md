**The short answer:** [ACOG advises keeping caffeine below 200 mg per day during pregnancy](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/experts-and-stories/ask-acog/how-much-coffee-can-i-drink-while-pregnant). Count caffeine from all sources, not just coffee. A cup's size and the way it is prepared can change the amount substantially.

## Does one cup of coffee fit under 200 mg?

Maybe. The [FDA's typical ranges](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) put a **12-fluid-ounce brewed coffee at 113–247 mg**. That range crosses ACOG's daily guide, so “one cup” is not a reliable measurement. Check the serving size and caffeine amount supplied by the shop or manufacturer when available. A large or strong coffee may use more of the day's caffeine than expected.

This is a practical limit for planning, not a claim that 199 mg is risk-free or 200 mg is harmful. The [NIH pregnancy supplement review](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/) notes that ACOG does not find moderate caffeine intake to be a major contributor to miscarriage or preterm birth, while the relationship with fetal growth remains uncertain. Discuss your own circumstances with your prenatal clinician.

## What else counts toward the day?

Coffee is only one source. For **12-fluid-ounce drinks**, the FDA gives these typical amounts:

| Drink | Typical caffeine |
| --- | ---: |
| Brewed coffee | 113–247 mg |
| Black tea | 71 mg |
| Green tea | 37 mg |
| Caffeinated soft drink | 23–83 mg |
| Energy drink | 41–246 mg |

These are estimates, not guaranteed amounts for a particular product. The FDA also identifies chocolate, some energy or protein bars, dietary supplements, and over-the-counter medicines as possible caffeine sources. Some supplement ingredients, such as guarana, contain caffeine; the [NIH review](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/) notes that the amount can be hard to determine from a supplement label. Ask your pharmacist or prenatal clinician about a product when the label is unclear.

PregNut's [food nutrition lookup](/food/) compares selected nutrients per 100 grams; **it does not measure caffeine**. Do not use its coffee, tea, or chocolate entries to estimate your daily caffeine intake.

## Is decaf caffeine-free?

No. The [FDA says an 8-fluid-ounce decaf coffee typically contains 2–15 mg](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much). Decaf can lower your intake, but it still belongs in the daily total if you are counting closely.

## A simple way to count it

1. Write down each caffeinated drink, food, supplement, or medicine you use that day.
2. Record the actual serving size and the product's caffeine amount when provided.
3. Add the amounts together. If the amount is missing, ask the maker or a health professional rather than treating it as zero.

If caffeine worsens nausea, sleep, or palpitations, raise that with your clinician. The [FDA recommends cutting back gradually](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much) if you regularly consume caffeine, because withdrawal can be unpleasant.
