**The short answer:** Fish, potatoes, bananas, and other foods can contribute vitamin B6 to your diet. [NIH lists 1.9 mg per day](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB6-HealthProfessional/) as the recommended amount during pregnancy. That nutrition target is a different question from using a B6 supplement to treat nausea. Eating a B6-containing food has not been shown to replace nausea treatment.

## How much B6 is in familiar foods?

PregNut's [USDA-based food data](/about/) reports these amounts **per 100 g of the food as named**:

| Food | Vitamin B6 per 100 g | What the entry describes |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| [Cooked sockeye salmon](/food/15086-salmon-sockeye-cooked-dry-heat/) | **0.82 mg** | Salmon cooked with dry heat. |
| [Boiled potato, flesh](/food/11831-potatoes-boiled-cooked-in-skin-flesh/) | **0.29 mg** | Potato cooked in its skin; flesh only in the data. |
| [Raw banana](/food/09040-bananas-raw/) | **0.36 mg** | Edible banana, without the peel. |
| [Raw avocado](/food/09037-avocados-raw/) | **0.25 mg** | Edible avocado flesh. |

These are equal-weight comparisons, **not serving recommendations**. For everyday portions, the [NIH food table](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB6-HealthProfessional/) lists about **0.6 mg in 3 ounces of cooked sockeye salmon**, **0.4 mg in 1 cup of boiled potatoes**, and **0.4 mg in a medium banana**. Actual portions, varieties, and preparation can change the numbers. If you eat fish, use our [lower-mercury fish guide](/blog/low-mercury-fish-pregnancy/) for serving and safety context.

## Does eating B6-rich food treat morning sickness?

Food can help you meet your usual nutrient needs, but the evidence for treating nausea concerns **B6 supplements**, not a food-based B6 target. [ACOG discusses vitamin B6 as an over-the-counter treatment](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/morning-sickness-nausea-and-vomiting-of-pregnancy) for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy. [NIH notes mixed trial results](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB6-HealthProfessional/). Neither source shows that eating a banana, potato, or salmon treats nausea.

If nausea makes eating difficult, choose foods you can tolerate. ACOG suggests small, frequent meals and bland foods if they help. If you cannot keep liquids down, feel faint when standing, or have very little dark urine, [contact your pregnancy care team](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/morning-sickness-nausea-and-vomiting-of-pregnancy/) rather than trying to solve dehydration with a food ranking.

For a separate option, read what the evidence says about [ginger tea and supplements for pregnancy nausea](/blog/ginger-tea-capsules-pregnancy-nausea/).

## Should I add a B6 supplement?

Check the vitamin B6 amount and serving size on your prenatal's **Supplement Facts** panel, then discuss any additional B6 or nausea treatment with your clinician. [NIH advises consulting a physician before taking B6 for pregnancy nausea](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB6-HealthProfessional/) because treatment doses can approach intake limits. High supplemental intakes over time can harm nerves; ordinary food amounts have not been reported to cause that problem. Our [prenatal label guide](/blog/prenatal-vitamin-label-pregnancy/) can help you check what is already in your product.

## Common questions

### Is the 1.9 mg daily amount a nausea-treatment dose?

No. It is the **recommended dietary allowance during pregnancy**, a nutrition reference for usual intake. ACOG's discussion of B6 for nausea concerns treatment. Ask your clinician which approach is appropriate for your symptoms and what your prenatal already supplies.

### Is a banana enough B6 for the whole day?

NIH lists about **0.4 mg in a medium banana**, part of the **1.9 mg daily pregnancy reference**. Other foods and your prenatal may contribute too. A banana is not a stand-alone treatment for nausea.

### Can I use the food chart to pick a safe fish?

No. Nutrient amount is only one consideration. Check the [FDA/EPA fish chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) for mercury guidance and [CDC's pregnancy food-safety advice](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) for preparation; choose fully cooked fish.
