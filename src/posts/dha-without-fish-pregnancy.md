**The short answer:** If you eat little or no fish, check whether your prenatal lists **DHA in milligrams per serving** and discuss your overall omega-3 intake with your prenatal clinician. “Fish oil,” “total omega-3,” EPA, and DHA are different label lines. [NIH explains](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) that no U.S. recommended dietary allowance has been established specifically for DHA.

## What if I do eat fish?

The [FDA/EPA fish chart](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) recommends **two to three adult servings a week** from its lower-mercury **Best Choices** category during pregnancy. An adult serving is **4 ounces, measured before cooking**. Salmon, sardines, shrimp, and canned light tuna are examples; the species and preparation matter. See our [lower-mercury fish guide](/blog/low-mercury-fish-pregnancy/) before using a nutrient ranking to choose seafood.

## How do I compare DHA on supplement labels?

1. **Start with serving size.** The Supplement Facts amounts may describe more than one capsule or gummy.
2. **Find DHA itself.** A label's fish-oil amount is not its DHA amount. NIH gives an example of a typical **1,000 mg fish-oil** supplement with about **120 mg DHA** and **180 mg EPA**; products vary widely.
3. **Read EPA separately.** DHA and EPA are both long-chain omega-3 fats, but a combined EPA-plus-DHA number does not tell you the DHA amount by itself.
4. **Check your prenatal too.** If you are considering another product, bring both labels to your clinician so the full supplement plan can be reviewed.

[NIH's omega-3 fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) describes fish-oil and algal-oil formulations and why the amounts must be read from the product label. Do not treat PregNut's **300 mg DHA chart benchmark** as a prescribed supplement dose or a U.S. DHA RDA.

## Are flax, chia, walnuts, or algal oil the same as DHA?

Flax, chia, and walnuts provide **ALA**, another omega-3 fat. The body can convert some ALA to EPA and DHA, but [NIH says the conversion is limited](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-Consumer/). Their ALA amount is not a DHA amount on a label.

**Algal oil** is a vegetarian source of DHA. NIH reports that algal products commonly provide about **100–300 mg DHA**, though formulas differ; read the DHA line on the actual product. Ask your clinician how a product fits with your food choices and prenatal, especially if you avoid all seafood.

## Can I compare a food with a capsule?

Yes, if you compare the **same nutrient and units**. PregNut's [USDA-based cooked sockeye salmon entry](/food/15086-salmon-sockeye-cooked-dry-heat/) lists **0.56 g DHA per 100 g**, which is **560 mg DHA per 100 g**. A supplement label usually lists **mg per labeled serving**. Those are different portion sizes, and the salmon number does not account for fish safety or tell you which supplement to take.

## Common questions

### Does my prenatal need to contain DHA?

Prenatal formulas differ. Read your label and discuss your diet and supplement choices with your care team; a food chart cannot decide what your individual plan needs.

### Does an omega-3 supplement replace eating fish?

It may provide DHA or EPA, depending on its label, but fish supplies other nutrients too. If you do eat fish, follow the [FDA/EPA lower-mercury and serving guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish). If you do not, ask your clinician about an appropriate food and supplement plan.

### Is 300 mg DHA a pregnancy requirement?

No. That is PregNut's illustrative comparison benchmark. NIH says no U.S. DHA RDA has been set. Do not use the site's chart percentage to choose a dose.
