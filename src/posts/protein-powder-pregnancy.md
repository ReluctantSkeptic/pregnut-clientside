**The short answer:** A protein powder's name or protein number alone cannot tell you whether that specific product fits your pregnancy diet. Check its **amount per labeled serving**, its full ingredient list, and any added vitamins, minerals, or botanicals alongside your prenatal. The [FDA advises discussing dietary supplements with a health professional](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/fda-101-dietary-supplements) before using them; bring the actual package or a photo of its label to your prenatal clinician or dietitian.

## Why is PregNut's 100 g number different from a scoop?

PregNut's food pages use **100 grams of powder** so foods can be compared on the same basis. A scoop is whatever amount the current product label defines; it is not necessarily 100 g. For example, our reference entries list [whey-based powder](/food/14066-protein-powder-whey-based/) at **78.13 g protein per 100 g** and [soy-based powder](/food/14067-protein-powder-soy-based/) at **55.56 g per 100 g**. A *hypothetical measured 30 g portion* of those entries works out to about **23.4 g** and **16.7 g** protein, respectively. That arithmetic is not a serving claim for a product sold today.

Use the **serving size and protein amount on your own package** for a real choice. Formula, scoop size, and preparation can differ from a database entry. The [milk-based protein supplement page](/food/01223-protein-supplement-milk-based-muscle-milk-powder/) is likewise a reference food, not a review or endorsement of a current brand.

## What should I check on the label?

1. **Serving size and protein:** Read grams of powder per serving and grams of protein in that serving. The [FDA's label guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling) explains the **Supplement Facts** panel used for dietary supplements; other protein products may use **Nutrition Facts**.
2. **Added nutrients:** Compare added vitamins and minerals with your prenatal and other supplements. More of every nutrient is not automatically helpful; [NIH's pregnancy supplement review](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/) describes recommended intakes and upper limits. Use our [prenatal label guide](/blog/prenatal-vitamin-label-pregnancy/) to make a side-by-side list.
3. **Other ingredients:** Check for botanicals or other added ingredients beyond protein. NIH notes that most botanical supplements lack good pregnancy safety and efficacy evidence. Ask about the *specific ingredient and amount* rather than assuming that every “protein powder” has the same formula.

FDA does not approve dietary supplements for safety and effectiveness before they are sold. That regulatory fact does not determine whether an individual product is suitable for you; it is a reason to use the actual label and your care team's advice rather than a generic powder name.

## Do I need a powder to get enough protein?

Not necessarily. [ACOG recommends a variety of protein foods](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy). Beans, tofu, yogurt, eggs, meat, and fish can all contribute when they fit your diet and pregnancy food-safety guidance. Our [vegetarian protein guide](/blog/vegetarian-protein-pregnancy/) compares familiar food portions. PregNut's **70 g daily chart benchmark** is a comparison aid, not an individualized prescription; ask your clinician or dietitian about your needs if eating enough is difficult or your diet is restricted.
