**The short answer:** If meals bring on heartburn, try eating **smaller amounts more often** and notice which foods or drinks make *your* symptoms worse. Stay upright after eating and leave time between your evening meal and bed. These steps appear in [ACOG's reflux guidance](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/problems-of-the-digestive-system) and the [NHS pregnancy heartburn guide](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/indigestion-and-heartburn/). There is no universal list of reflux triggers that everyone must avoid.

## How can I adjust meals without cutting out too much?

Start with the **size and timing** of a meal. The NHS suggests small meals often instead of three larger meals, sitting upright while eating and afterward, and avoiding food within three hours of bedtime. ACOG also recommends smaller, more frequent meals and not lying down right after eating.

For example, you could divide a larger lunch into two smaller portions and save the second for later. Choose foods you already tolerate and keep the rest of your pregnancy nutrition plan in view. PregNut's [food lookup](/food/) can compare nutrients in foods, but its per-100-gram charts **cannot predict whether a food will trigger reflux**.

## Which foods and drinks should I watch?

[ACOG notes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/problems-of-the-digestive-system) that caffeine, citrus, tomatoes, and fried foods can worsen reflux for some people. The [NHS also suggests reducing](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/indigestion-and-heartburn/) rich, spicy, or fatty foods and caffeine if they aggravate symptoms. Notice what happens after *your* meals before removing a broad food group. If coffee or tea is a trigger, our [caffeine guide](/blog/caffeine-coffee-tea-pregnancy/) helps you count it from drinks and other sources.

A simple note of **what you ate, when, how much, and when symptoms appeared** can make patterns easier to discuss with your prenatal clinician. An occasional symptom after one meal does not prove that a food is unsafe in pregnancy. Pregnancy food safety is a separate question; see our [food-safety guide](/blog/food-safety-pregnancy/) for preparation and storage advice.

## What if food changes are not enough?

Tell your prenatal clinician or pharmacist if heartburn persists or disrupts eating or sleep. The [NHS pregnancy guide](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/indigestion-and-heartburn/) discusses medicines that may help and says some antacids are unsuitable during pregnancy. It also advises **not taking antacids within two hours of folic acid or iron supplements** because absorption can be affected. Bring your prenatal and medicine labels when asking about options; our [prenatal-label guide](/blog/prenatal-vitamin-label-pregnancy/) can help you find the amounts already in your product.

Seek medical advice if you have difficulty swallowing, weight loss, persistent cough or hoarseness, or pain or swelling in your stomach, as the NHS recommends. Do not assume a new or severe symptom is ordinary heartburn.

## Common questions

### Are spicy foods always off limits?

No. Spicy foods can worsen symptoms for some people. If they do not bother you, this guide is not a reason to exclude them.

### Will more calcium in a food stop heartburn?

PregNut's calcium number describes nutrient content, not reflux relief. A food's nutrient ranking cannot show whether it will ease your symptoms.
