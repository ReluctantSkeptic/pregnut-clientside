**The short answer:** Yes, store-bought ranch dressing is safe during pregnancy. The [FDA says](https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/dairy-and-eggs-food-safety-moms-be) commercial mayonnaise, dressings, and sauces contain **pasteurized eggs** that are safe to eat. The ranch to ask about is **homemade or fresh-made** ranch that could contain raw egg or unpasteurized dairy.

## Is bottled ranch dressing safe while pregnant?

Yes. FDA's statement covers **commercial dressings** as a category, so a bottle from the grocery store fits it whatever the brand. The [CDC chart](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) likewise names pasteurized eggs as the safer choice in foods that will not be cooked, such as salad dressing. Refrigerate the bottle after opening as the label directs.

## Is pasteurized ranch dressing something to look for?

You do not need to hunt for the word on a bottled ranch; FDA's commercial-dressing statement already covers the eggs. The dairy is a separate check. Ranch is usually made with buttermilk, sour cream, or both, and [CDC lists dairy products made from pasteurized milk](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) as the safer choice and raw-milk dairy as the riskier one. [FDA advises](https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/dairy-and-eggs-food-safety-moms-be) making sure the dairy you eat is made with pasteurized milk and checking the label, especially on farm-made or locally made products.

## Is restaurant ranch OK when pregnant?

Usually, but ask if it is **made in-house**. A house-made ranch may use fresh mayonnaise or aioli, and the [FDA lists homemade mayonnaise and fresh-made dressings made with raw eggs](https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/dairy-and-eggs-food-safety-moms-be) among foods to avoid during pregnancy. If the kitchen uses store-bought mayonnaise and pasteurized buttermilk or sour cream, it fits the safer choices. If no one is sure, ask for a bottled dressing instead. Our [mayonnaise guide](/blog/mayonnaise-pregnancy/) covers aioli and restaurant sauces.

## Can I make ranch at home while pregnant?

Yes. Mixing a ranch seasoning packet or your own herbs with **store-bought mayonnaise** and **pasteurized** buttermilk or sour cream keeps every ingredient in the safer column. If a recipe calls for a raw egg, the [CDC names pasteurized eggs](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/pregnant-women.html) as the safer choice for foods that will not be cooked. Our [sour cream guide](/blog/sour-cream-pregnancy/) covers label checks for the dairy.

## How long can ranch sit out?

Keep ranch refrigerated at **40°F or below**. At a party, wing night, or picnic, FDA's [two-hour rule](https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/ready-eat-foods-food-safety-moms-be) applies: discard perishable food left at room temperature for more than **2 hours**, or more than **1 hour** when it is above 90°F. A bowl of ranch dip that sat out all afternoon is the one to skip.

## Common questions

### Does ranch dressing have any nutrients that matter in pregnancy?

Not many per spoonful. PregNut's historical USDA-based data lists [regular ranch dressing](/food/04639-salad-dressing-ranch-regular/) at **430 calories** and **32.8 mg choline** per 100 g, so it is mainly a source of fat and calories. A typical serving is much smaller than 100 g.

### What about ranch dip, blue cheese, or Caesar dressing?

Store-bought versions follow the same FDA statement about commercial dressings. For blue cheese dressing, the cheese should also be made with pasteurized milk; our [soft cheese guide](/blog/soft-cheese-pregnancy/) explains why. Fresh-made Caesar dressing at a restaurant is the one to ask about, since FDA lists it among raw-egg dressings.

### What if I ate homemade ranch while pregnant?

Tell your prenatal care team if you are worried. The [CDC's symptom guide](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/signs-symptoms/index.html) lists *Salmonella*, which is linked to raw eggs, as starting **6 hours to 6 days** after eating, and says to see a doctor if you are pregnant and have a fever with other flu-like symptoms.
