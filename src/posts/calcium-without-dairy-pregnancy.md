**The short answer:** You can get calcium without dairy. Fortified drinks, calcium-set tofu, canned fish with edible bones, and some vegetables can all contribute. The [NIH pregnancy reference](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/) is **1,000 mg per day for ages 19–50** and **1,300 mg for ages 14–18**. Your usual portions and the rest of your diet matter more than one high-ranking food.

## Three nondairy comparisons

These are entries in [PregNut's USDA-based food data](/about/), each measured per **100 g**. They are examples, not interchangeable serving sizes:

- [Canned Atlantic sardines, drained, with bones](/food/15088-sardine-atlantic-canned-in-oil-drained-solids-with-bone/): **382 mg calcium**. The edible bones matter; a boneless fish entry is different. Sardines are among the [FDA/EPA lower-mercury choices](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish).
- [Soy milk with added calcium and vitamins A and D](/food/16139-soymilk-original-and-vanilla-with-added-calcium-vitamins-a-and-d/): **123 mg calcium**. Fortification varies by product, so check the carton instead of assuming every soy milk matches this entry.
- [Soft tofu prepared with calcium sulfate and nigari](/food/16127-tofu-soft-prepared-with-calcium-sulfate-and-magnesium-chloride-nigari/): **111 mg calcium**. Tofu made with a different coagulant may contain much less.

Vegetables such as kale and broccoli contribute too. [NIH notes](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/) that calcium absorption differs among foods: spinach may look substantial on a nutrient chart, but much less of its calcium is absorbed. Compare the food you actually buy and eat, then use [the food lookup](/food/) to estimate its contribution.

## What if my prenatal contains calcium?

Read the **Supplement Facts serving size** and calcium amount. NIH reports that prenatal products often contain relatively little calcium. A prenatal label does not tell you how much you get from meals, and a 100 g food comparison cannot diagnose a shortfall. If you avoid dairy or several other calcium sources, bring a typical day's foods and your prenatal label to your clinician or dietitian before adding a separate supplement. [Vitamin D also helps calcium absorption](/blog/vitamin-d-foods-pregnancy/). If dairy is absent from your diet, [check iodine sources](/blog/iodine-without-dairy-pregnancy/) and [riboflavin foods](/blog/riboflavin-without-dairy-pregnancy/) too.
