Three pregnancies, one food database.

Yuriy StasyukBuilding PregNut since 2018

I am fairly confident that no grocery list in history has been cross-examined as thoroughly as ours was in 2018.

That year my wife became pregnant with our first child, and I wanted to do the one useful thing a nervous husband could do: make sure the food she ate carried as much of what she and the baby needed as possible.

Expectant parents are said to "nest", readying the house for someone who has not arrived yet. Mine took a less photogenic form, which I can only describe as data nesting. Instead of painting a nursery, I downloaded the government's food composition database. (USDA, if you are reading this, you deserve more fan mail than you get.)

Most of the advice I found was a list. A list tells you what to eat; a comparison tells you why one food beats another. So I built the comparison, measuring every food "per 100 grams", which simply means the same weight of everything, so spinach and salmon compete on equal terms.

My aim was not to out-think her doctor, nor to turn dinner into a math problem. It was to make the good choices a little easier to see.

2018

The first version, built from USDA data for our first pregnancy. Rough, useful, and written for an audience of exactly two.

Then a second.

Expecting again, I came back with a longer list of questions and kept adding to it.

And a third.

Now it covers 1,272 foods, 13 nutrients and every week of pregnancy, with guides that link straight to their sources.

The fine print.

Where the numbers come from

Food values come from a fixed snapshot of the USDA National Nutrient Database for Standard Reference (see the SR Legacy archive). USDA's final Standard Reference release was in 2018, so newer data and current product labels may differ. Names are tidied for reading; every value describes 100 grams, not a brand or a serving.

What the percentages compare against

Most reference amounts come from the NIH pregnancy intake table for ages 19–50. Choline and potassium are adequate intakes; DHA and protein are illustrative benchmarks. Folate is shown as DFE, which differs from the folic acid on a prenatal label (folate guide). The data has no iodine values (iodine guide).

Who writes this

PregNut is an independent project by Yuriy Stasyuk. It is a starting point for your own research, not medical advice, and it does not replace your obstetric clinician or a registered dietitian. Some guides were drafted with AI assistance and edited before publication; each links its sources, and medical review is credited only when it has happened. Spot a wrong number or an outdated source? Tell me.

What began as one worried project is, it seems, a family tradition.Yuriy
